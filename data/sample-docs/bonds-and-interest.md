# Bonds and Interest Rates

A bond is a loan that an investor makes to a government or a company. The borrower promises to pay interest, called the coupon, at regular intervals, and to return the principal, the amount originally borrowed, when the bond matures. A government bond is generally considered the safest type of bond, because a government can raise taxes to repay its debts, while a corporate bond carries the risk that the company defaults, fails to pay. Investors demand higher yields from riskier borrowers to compensate for that default risk.

## Yield and price

A bond's yield is the annual return an investor earns, and it moves in the opposite direction to the bond's price. When interest rates rise, new bonds pay more, so existing bonds with lower coupons become less attractive and their prices fall. When interest rates fall, existing bonds become more attractive and their prices rise. This inverse relationship between price and yield is the most important fact about bonds. A bond bought at a discount to its face value earns a yield higher than its coupon rate.

## Inflation and real returns

Inflation reduces the real value of a bond's fixed payments. If a bond pays 4% interest while inflation is 3%, the real return is only about 1%, because the money repaid buys less than the money lent. This is why inflation is the main enemy of bond investors. Inflation-linked bonds, whose payments rise with inflation, exist precisely to protect against this. The real return, the return after inflation, is the number that matters for long-term wealth, not the nominal return printed on the bond.

## Central banks and duration

Central banks, such as the Federal Reserve in the United States, set short-term interest rates to manage inflation and employment. When inflation is high, central banks raise rates to slow borrowing and spending; when the economy is weak, they cut rates to encourage borrowing and spending. Duration measures how sensitive a bond's price is to changes in interest rates: a bond with a duration of 8 years loses roughly 8% of its value if rates rise by 1%, while a bond with a duration of 2 years loses roughly 2%. Long-duration bonds are more volatile, but they also gain more when rates fall.
