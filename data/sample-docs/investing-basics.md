# Investing Basics

Investing means putting money into assets such as stocks, bonds, or funds with the expectation that they will grow in value over time. The two ideas that matter most for a beginner are compound interest and diversification. Compound interest is the growth of money on top of money already earned: returns are reinvested and themselves earn returns, so wealth grows faster the longer it stays invested. Starting early matters more than picking the perfect investment, because time in the market lets compounding do most of the work.

## Index funds and diversification

An index fund is a fund that tracks a whole market index, such as the S&P 500, by holding the same stocks in the same proportions. Because an index fund holds hundreds of companies, a single company failing barely affects it. This is diversification: spreading money across many assets so that no single failure can destroy the portfolio. Diversification does not remove risk entirely, because whole markets can fall, but it removes the risk of one company ruining your savings. For most beginners, a low-cost diversified index fund is the simplest sensible investment.

## Risk and asset allocation

Risk in investing means the chance that returns differ from what you expect, including losing money. Stocks are higher risk and higher expected return; bonds are lower risk and lower expected return; cash is the safest and earns the least. Asset allocation is the split of a portfolio between these asset classes. A young investor with a long horizon can usually hold more stocks, because they have time to recover from market falls. Someone close to retirement usually holds more bonds, because they cannot afford a large fall just before they need the money.

## Dollar-cost averaging

Dollar-cost averaging means investing a fixed amount of money at regular intervals, for example every month, regardless of what the market is doing. When prices are high, the fixed amount buys fewer shares; when prices are low, it buys more shares. This removes the need to time the market, which even professionals fail at consistently, and it turns market falls into an advantage for the regular buyer. The discipline of investing the same amount every month matters more than guessing the right day to buy.

## Fees and inflation

Fees quietly destroy returns. A fund that charges 1% per year takes a large share of your wealth over decades, while an index fund charging 0.05% per year takes almost nothing. Always check the expense ratio before buying a fund. Inflation is the other silent cost: if prices rise 3% per year and your money earns 2%, your purchasing power falls even though the number in your account grows. The goal of investing is not to make the number bigger, but to make your purchasing power grow faster than inflation.
